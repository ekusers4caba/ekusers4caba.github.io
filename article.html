    By Christopher Abraham 26/09/2023

    An online presence has become part of the modern CV.
    A CareerBuilder survey found that 70 percent of employers use social media to screen candidates, and 54 percent found content that caused them not to hire someone.

    Having no presence carries risk too, because 47 percent of employers are less likely to interview a candidate they cannot find online. Management writer Tom Peters framed the idea in 1997: "We are CEOs of our own companies: Me Inc."



    Establishing a presence usually begins with an audit: searching one's own name, tightening privacy settings and deciding what the individual wants to be known for.

    Next come consistent usernames, a professional photograph and a short biography across platforms. Consistency matters because employers often cross-check what a CV claims against what appears online, so profiles should tell the same story.

    The final piece is a central hub, ideally a personal website that the individual controls.

    Choosing a Platform

    Platforms are best chosen by audience rather than fashion. Two or three well-maintained profiles generally outperform many neglected ones. Each platform serves a different purpose: professional networks build credibility, portfolios demonstrate skill, and social channels show personality and communication style.


    LinkedIn is the default for recruiters in almost every sector.

    A personal website provides a permanent, searchable portfolio.

    Industry-specific platforms serve particular fields. GitHub suits computing, Behance and Dribbble suit design, and YouTube or Instagram suit media, photography and marketing. Academic and journalism fields often favour X or Bluesky.

    Promoting Work and Getting Noticed

    Promotion is largely consistent, generous participation. Common steps include:

    • Sharing projects as short stories covering the problem, the process and the result.

    • Commenting thoughtfully on others' work and joining industry groups.

    • Following target companies and engaging with their content before applying.

    • Using clear keywords in headlines and biographies so recruiters can find profiles.

    • Collecting recommendations from lecturers, placement supervisors and clients.

    • Including portfolio links on CVs and in every application.

    Standing Out From the Competition

    Graduates in the same field often share similar grades and skills, so distinction comes from focus and evidence. Peters suggested that individuals ask what their "product" does that makes it different.

    A clear one-sentence positioning statement helps, such as "a data analyst who communicates through visual storytelling." Evidence beats adjectives: a measurable result, such as improving a website's loading time or growing a society's membership, says more than describing oneself as hardworking.

    Unusual combinations of strengths, self-initiated projects, volunteering and competition entries all signal initiative. A consistent voice and a defined specialism make a candidate memorable.

    What to Include and What to Exclude

    Include:

    • A small selection of the best three to six pieces, chosen for quality over quantity.

    • Case studies explaining the individual's role, tools, decisions and outcomes.

    • Testimonials, certificates and links to live or published work.

    • A concise, professional "About" section.

    Exclude:

    • Weak, outdated or unfinished work.

    • Confidential client or placement material shared without permission.

    • Content that employers in the survey flagged, such as provocative posts, references to drink or drugs, and discriminatory comments.

    • Complaints about lecturers, employers or colleagues.

    Update Frequency and Examples of Work

    A portfolio is typically refreshed after each significant project and reviewed every term. On the main platform, posting or engaging two or three times a week is a sustainable rhythm, since regularity matters more than volume.

    Metrics such as profile views and post engagement can indicate what is working. As personal branding author Dan Schawbel puts it, "Life is one big pitch, so you'd better start practicing."


    Showcasing can begin early, even with limited experience. Typical examples include:

    • A coursework project rewritten as a case study.

    • A GitHub repository with a clear README.

    • A big-worded blog post analysing an industry trend.

    • Before-and-after screenshots or a two-minute walkthrough video.

    • A self-initiated redesign or improvement for a local business or community group.

    Industry Guidance and Advice for Students

    Across most professions, the guidance is consistent: be professional, visible and authentic. Following professional bodies and leading practitioners helps students learn the standards and vocabulary of their field.

    Many industries also publish codes of conduct or social media guidelines, and following these protects both the individual and future employers. Keeping a portfolio folder up to date means evidence is ready whenever opportunities appear, and treating each assignment as a potential showcase piece builds material steadily.

    General guidelines for students include:


    • Searching one's own name regularly.

    • Separating personal and professional accounts where possible.

    • Building contacts among alumni and industry speakers.

    • Seeking permission before sharing group work.

    • Proofreading all public content.

    • Using a professional email address and profile picture.

    • Being honest about skills and experience.

    • Keeping content positive and constructive.

    • Being consistent in tone, style and posting frequency.

    • Using analytics to learn what content resonates with the audience.

    • Being patient: building a presence takes time, and quality matters more than quantity.

    None of these rules make a game easy. They make it honest, and honest difficulty is what keeps players coming back for one more attempt.
