    By Christopher Abraham 04/10/2026

    Last night I had the chance to attend Glasgow TennoVIP 2026, an evening that turned out to be one of the most valuable experiences I've had so far as an aspiring game developer.

    TennoVIP is Digital Extremes' community event series built around Warframe, and this particular evening brought together fans, developers, and members of the community team for an evening of conversation, insight, and genuine connection.

    For someone still early in their journey into the games industry, it was a rare opportunity to speak directly with people who have built careers at a studio I deeply admire.

    The Conversation

    The highlight of the night was an insightful discussion with members of the Warframe development and community team about a question that matters a great deal to junior developers and students: how do you stand out and actually break into a studio like Digital Extremes?

    A huge thank you to Megan Everett, Adrian Bott, and Zach McKone, who each took the time to share honest, practical advice on getting noticed by employers and building a path into video game development.

    What We Talked About

    A few of the key themes from the conversation:

    • Building a visible portfolio — having finished, playable work online matters more than a long list of unfinished ideas.
    • Getting noticed by studios — engaging genuinely with a studio's community, events and output before ever applying.
    • Standing out from other candidates — leaning into a specific skill or niche rather than trying to do everything.
    • Networking honestly — treating events like this as a conversation, not a pitch, and following up afterwards.
    • Persistence over perfection — most people's path into the industry wasn't linear, and that's normal.

    Why Moments Like This Matter

    What struck me most was how generous the conversation felt. There was no gatekeeping or vague platitudes, just real, actionable insight from people who clearly care about helping the next generation of developers find their footing.

    Moments like these are invaluable for anyone starting out in games, where the path in isn't always obvious and advice from people who've actually walked it is worth more than almost anything else.

    Final Thoughts

    I'm incredibly grateful for the opportunity, and for the generosity of the Digital Extremes team in hosting an event like this close to home. It's one thing to admire a studio from a distance; it's another to be welcomed in, even briefly, and treated as part of the conversation rather than an outsider looking in.

    To anyone else studying or starting out in game development: look out for events like this. The advice is useful, but the connections and confidence you take away from them matter just as much.

    By Christopher Abraham 26/09/2023

    An online presence has become part of the modern CV. Research shows around 70% of employers use social media to screen candidates, and 47% are less likely to interview someone they cannot find online at all. Management writer Tom Peters put the underlying idea plainly back in 1997: "We are CEOs of our own companies: Me Inc."

    Establishing a presence usually begins with an audit: searching your own name, tightening privacy settings, and deciding what you actually want to be known for. Next comes consistency, the same username, a professional photo, and a short bio that tells the same story everywhere it appears. The final piece is a central hub, ideally a personal website you control, that everything else points back to.

    Choosing a Platform

    Platforms are best chosen by audience rather than fashion. Two or three well-maintained profiles generally outperform many neglected ones, and each serves a different purpose: professional networks build credibility, portfolios demonstrate skill, and social channels show personality and communication style.

    • LinkedIn is the default for recruiters in almost every sector.
    • A personal website provides a permanent, searchable portfolio you fully control.
    • Industry-specific platforms serve particular fields; GitHub for computing, Behance and Dribbble for design, YouTube or Instagram for media and photography.

    Promoting Work and Getting Noticed

    Promotion is largely consistent, generous participation rather than any single clever trick. Common steps include:

    • Sharing projects as short stories covering the problem, the process, and the result.
    • Commenting thoughtfully on others' work and joining relevant industry groups.
    • Following target companies and engaging with their content before applying.
    • Using clear, specific keywords in headlines and bios so recruiters can actually find you.
    • Collecting recommendations from tutors, placement supervisors, and clients.
    • Including a portfolio link on every CV and application.

    Standing Out From the Competition

    Graduates in the same field often share similar grades and skills, so distinction comes from focus and evidence. Peters suggested asking what your "product" does that makes it different, in fifteen words or less. As one design agency put it when reviewing portfolios: "I would much rather see five strong pieces than sift through twenty mediocre ones."

    A clear one-sentence positioning statement helps, something like "a data analyst who communicates through visual storytelling." Evidence beats adjectives: a measurable result, such as improving a site's load time or growing a society's membership, says more than calling yourself hardworking. Self-initiated projects, volunteering, and competition entries all signal initiative on top of coursework.

    What to Include and What to Exclude

    Include:

    • A small selection of your best three to six pieces, chosen for quality over quantity.
    • Case studies explaining your role, tools, decisions, and outcomes.
    • Testimonials, certificates, and links to live or published work.
    • A concise, professional "About" section.

    Exclude:

    • Weak, outdated, or unfinished work.
    • Confidential client or placement material shared without permission.
    • Content that surveyed employers flag provocative posts, references to drink or drugs, discriminatory comments.
    • Complaints about tutors, employers, or colleagues.

    Update Frequency and Examples of Work

    A portfolio is typically refreshed after each significant project and reviewed every term. On your main platform, posting or engaging two or three times a week is a sustainable rhythm, since regularity matters more than volume. As personal branding writer Dan Schawbel puts it, "Life is one big pitch, so you'd better start practicing."

    Showcasing can begin early, even with limited experience. Typical starter examples include:

    • A coursework project rewritten as a short case study.
    • A GitHub repository with a clear, well-written README.
    • A blog post analysing a current trend in your field.
    • Before-and-after screenshots or a two-minute walkthrough video.
    • A self-initiated redesign or improvement for a local business or society.

    Industry Guidance and Advice for Students

    Across most professions the guidance is consistent: be professional, visible, and authentic. Following professional bodies and leading practitioners helps you learn the standards and vocabulary of your field, and many industries publish their own social media guidelines worth reading early.

    • Search your own name regularly.
    • Separate personal and professional accounts where possible.
    • Build contacts among alumni and industry speakers.
    • Seek permission before sharing group or client work.
    • Proofread everything before it goes public.
    • Use a professional email address and profile photo.
    • Be honest about your skills and experience.
    • Keep content constructive rather than critical.
    • Stay consistent in tone, style, and posting frequency.
    • Be patient, a presence takes time, and quality still beats quantity.

    By Christopher Abraham 04/10/2026

    Creative industries are often defined by talent, originality and vision. Yet employers and clients tend to judge practitioners on other criteria: reliability, communication and conduct.

    This post examines whether the concept of professionalism applies to creative work, drawing on the pillars of commitment, integrity, responsibility and accountability, as well as established codes of ethics, to argue that it is central to long-term success within the sector.

    Creativity Meets the Clock

    When I say I want to work in the creative industries, some people seem to assume the job is mostly inspiration, with deadlines and paperwork lurking somewhere in the background. I don't think that's true.

    Creative work is still work. It's usually made for someone else, to a deadline. So does professionalism apply to us? I think it does, and in this post I'll try to explain why, using the four pillars (commitment, integrity, responsibility and accountability) and a couple of real codes of ethics.

    Beyond the Dress Code: What Professionalism Means

    For me, professionalism is about being someone other people can rely on. It covers your skills, but it also covers how you behave when a project gets stressful. The four pillars are a handy way to break it down.

    Commitment is turning up and finishing what you said you'd finish. Integrity is being honest about what you can do and who did what. Responsibility is owning the quality of your work, including the bits that go wrong.

    Accountability is taking feedback and admitting mistakes instead of hoping nobody notices. None of this gets in the way of being creative. A brilliant illustrator who keeps missing deadlines won't be hired twice, and the same goes for a great developer who's hard to work with.

    Trust Is the Real Currency of Creative Work

    Hardly anything creative gets made alone. Designers, writers, developers and producers all depend on each other, and clients can't really judge what they're paying for until it lands, so a lot of it comes down to trust.

    The sector is big, too. According to the DCMS, the UK's creative industries produced £124.0 billion in GVA in 2023, which is 5.2% of the UK total, and just over half of that came from London. Figure 1 shows the split. When so much work is concentrated in a few places and networks, word gets around fast about who's good to work with.

    Why Integrity Is Worth the Bother

    Everyone's tempted to cut a corner at some point. Maybe it's using an asset without crediting it, claiming more experience with a tool than you really have, or pushing a website live without checking it's accessible.

    The trouble is that each of these chips away at how the whole discipline is seen. When clients get burned, everyone in the field feels it. So I think integrity protects other people's livelihoods as well as your own. Crediting is a good example, because the IGDA's crediting guidelines stress that accurate credit is central to developers' careers. One test I like is to ask whether I'd be happy for my client, my lecturer and a future employer to see exactly how I made something.

    Rules of the Road: Codes and Ethics

    Codes do exist, although they're not all equally formal. In web design and development, the W3C's Ethical Web Principles are a good starting point. They were published as a W3C Statement and include ideas like there being one web and the web being transparent. The W3C also talks about "promoting ethical thinking across the web industry".

    For games, the IGDA Code of Ethics says a code is "only as strong as the willingness of its members to uphold it", which I think is the key point about any code. It also lists discrimination and harassment as violations, and the IGDA has a formal process for investigating complaints.

    IGDA Scotland is a chapter of the IGDA, so the IGDA code is the one I'd point to for Scotland. Whatever your subject is, it's worth finding the equivalent and reading it before you need it.

    Be Someone People Want to Work With

    Respect is a big part of all this. Employers need to know you'll keep things confidential and do what you've said you'll do. Colleagues need credit for their work and honest, constructive feedback. Clients need realistic timelines and to be kept in the loop. The IGDA Foundation, for example, has a "zero-tolerance policy towards any kind of harassment or violence".

    Respect also means disagreeing properly: raising concerns early, criticising the work and not the person, and taking feedback without getting defensive. People who respect you tend to recommend you, even when you're not in the room.

    Your Name Walks Into the Room Before You Do

    In creative work, your portfolio might get you through the door, but your reputation decides what happens next. Jobs come from referrals, past clients and people you've collaborated with, and one missed deadline or public argument can be remembered for years. Social media makes this even more visible, since clients and recruiters often look you up before they reply.

    I see reputation as the four pillars viewed from the outside. People notice your commitment in whether you deliver, your integrity in how you credit others, your responsibility in the quality of the work and your accountability in how you handle mistakes.

    So my answer to the question is yes, professionalism does apply to the creative industries. It's what lets people trust, share and pay for creative work. For anyone starting out, that means crediting others, meeting deadlines, keeping your online presence honest and learning your discipline's code.
